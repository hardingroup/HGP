            01

            Calculus I

            Texas Tech · registered

            You ran the numbers through Calc III back at LCU — this is a re-wakening, not a cold start. Two big questions in a trench coat: how fast is something changing? (derivatives) and how much has piled up? (integrals). Warm back up on these before the semester lands.

            Warm-up · Algebra bridge

            Slope is the whole seed

            Calculus is built on one thing you already own: slope = rise over run. A straight line has one slope everywhere. Calculus asks the same question about curvy lines, where the slope keeps changing.

            slope m = (y2 − y1) / (x2 − x1)
            Idea 01 · Limits

            Sneaking up on a value

            A limit asks: as x creeps toward some number, what does f(x) head toward? It's the tool that makes "instantaneous" change legal. Read it as "the limit, as x approaches a, of f of x."

            limx→a f(x)
            Idea 02 · The derivative

            Slope of a curve, at one exact point

            The derivative is slope for curves — the rate of change at a single instant. It's the slope formula, shrunk with a limit until the two points collapse into one.

            f′(x) = limh→0 [ f(x+h) − f(x) ] / h
            Idea 03 · Power rule

            The shortcut you'll use constantly

            Once the limit above is proven, you rarely do it the long way. To differentiate x to a power: bring the exponent down front, then drop it by one.

            d/dx [ xn ] = n·x(n−1)

            Example: d/dx[x³] = 3x². That's it.

            Idea 04 · Integrals (the flip side)

            Adding up the accumulation

            An integral runs the derivative backward — instead of "how fast," it asks "how much total." Geometrically it's the area under the curve. You'll meet it after derivatives, but good to know the destination.

            ∫ f(x) dx

            Practice → Prove

            The practice exam is your rehearsal: 20 questions, no time limit, unlimited retakes — the real one is a single 90-minute attempt. Run the practice test until you're consistently above 50%, ideally into the 60%+ range that clears you straight into 1307. Videos teach it; the practice test proves it.             Score low? You're not stuck. ALEKS is a $30 online course that teaches from the ground up — hit 95% mastery any time in the 90-day window and you're eligible for 1307. Or take the CHEM 1301 prep course. Either way, there's a path in.

            Study Targets

            ranked by points ↓
            • P1
              Properties of atoms & molecules 15 q

              Nearly half the test — your #1 priority. Atomic structure (protons, neutrons, electrons), reading the periodic table, periodic trends (atomic size, metals vs. nonmetals across & down), electron arrangement, and ionic vs. covalent bonding.

            • P2
              Problem solving & algebra 7 q

              Your strong suit — you're about to take Calc I. Solving for unknowns, rearranging equations, ratios & proportions, scientific notation, and unit conversions (dimensional analysis). Nearly free points.

            • P3
              Formulas, naming & balancing 6 q

              Reading a formula like H₂O, naming basic compounds, and balancing equations — the LEGO-brick bookkeeping where atoms in = atoms out. The Crash Course video covers most of this.

            • P4
              Quantitative chemical problems 5 q

              The mole concept (a chemist's "dozen" — just a counting unit) and basic stoichiometry: using a balanced equation to find how much you get. Same stoichiometry video hits this directly.

            • P5
              Chemical reactions 2 q

              Lowest weight — skim this last. Just recognize basic reaction types: things combining, things breaking apart, things swapping partners.

            02

            Reading the Table

            navigate, don't memorize

            The periodic table is the best-organized warehouse ever built. Every element has a shelf location, and the location itself tells you what's on it. On the exam you'll have one in front of you — your job is to read it, not recall it.

            One box = one element

            A letter symbol (O, Na), a whole number = the atomic number (protons — the ID badge), and a decimal = the atomic mass. That's the whole box.

            Reads like text

            Left→right, top→bottom. The atomic number climbs by one each step — the table isn't random, it's counting.

            Columns = families

            Elements stacked in the same column behave alike — same number of outer electrons. Know one, and you roughly know the whole column.

            Metals vs. nonmetals

            Left & middle = metals (shiny, conduct). Upper right = nonmetals. A staircase line on the right divides them.

            Three neighborhoods

            Far-left = alkali metals (super reactive). Far-right = noble gases (loners). Just before them = halogens (reactive nonmetals).

            Trends move in directions

            Atoms get bigger down & left, smaller up & right. Position encodes behavior — exactly like a well-run warehouse.
